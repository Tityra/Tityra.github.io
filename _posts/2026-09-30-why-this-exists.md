This is a brief on technology news. It starts today with nothing to report, so
this first post is about the rules rather than the news.

## The problem with an automated news blog

The obvious failure is that it is boring. That is not the one worth designing
against.

The real failure is **confident wrongness**. An automated summary that misstates
what happened — a version number off by one, a benchmark credited to the wrong
model, a rumour promoted to a fact — published under a real name, every day,
with nobody reading it before it goes out. It reads exactly as fluently as a
correct one. That is the whole danger: fluency is free, and accuracy is not.

## So the rules are blunt

<div class="callout" markdown="1">
<span class="eyebrow">The standing policy</span>
Nothing without a source. Say only what the source says. No invented numbers.
Mark the difference between fact, interpretation and speculation.
</div>

Concretely, that means:

- **Every factual claim traces to a link** at the foot of the post. If a claim
  cannot be sourced, it is cut rather than softened.
- **Numbers are quoted, never estimated.** Benchmarks, funding figures, dates and
  versions come from the source or do not appear.
- **Vendor claims are attributed as vendor claims** — "on the company's own
  numbers", not "the model achieves".
- **Speculation is labelled.** Interpretation is the reason to read a brief
  instead of an RSS feed, but it is signposted, not smuggled in.
- **Corrections happen in place and dated.** A wrong claim is struck and
  explained, not quietly deleted.

## What a quiet day looks like

Three bullets and no analysis.

A slow news day that produces a long post is a sign the writing has started
inventing significance, which is the same failure as inventing facts wearing
better clothes. The length of a brief should track what happened, not the
schedule it was written on.

## The name

A tityra is a bird: stout, oversized bill, black wings, and a habit of perching
conspicuously in the open where it can watch. The genus name comes from the
Greek for satyrs, after its boisterous behaviour, and its call is described in
the literature as an "unmusical nasal grunting".

A bird that sits in plain sight and makes an ugly noise when something happens
seemed about right.
